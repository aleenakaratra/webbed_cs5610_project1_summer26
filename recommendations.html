Reading list

Books an AI thinks I should read

The rest of the site is dressed up like a metro line. This page is dressed up like a library. The assignment requires one AI-generated page, so I'm being literal about it — I told a language model what I like reading (long character-driven novels, slow-burn fantasy, the kind of fanfiction that takes itself seriously) and asked for ten suggestions. The list below is what it gave back. I haven't read most of them yet.

Generated using claude-opus-4-7, December 2025. Prompt and full transcript in the repo README.

  1. The Goblin Emperor — Katherine Addison

    A half-goblin teenager unexpectedly inherits an imperial throne and has to learn court politics from nothing. Quiet, kind, dense with names you'll need a glossary for.

  2. Piranesi — Susanna Clarke

    A man lives alone in an infinite house full of statues and tides. Short, strange, deeply felt. Reads in one sitting.

  3. A Memory Called Empire — Arkady Martine

    Space opera that's actually about being a small-state ambassador in a culturally overwhelming empire. The protagonist's grief is handled unusually well.

  4. The Locked Tomb series — Tamsyn Muir

    Necromancers in space, written like fan-fiction in the best sense — character voice and inside jokes drive the plot. Confusing on purpose.

  5. Babel — R. F. Kuang

    Translation as magic in an alternate-history Oxford. Polemical and angry; the politics are not subtle. Some readers love it, some don't.

  6. The Curse of Chalion — Lois McMaster Bujold

    Middle-aged courtier returns home, takes a tutoring job, becomes entangled in royal succession. Theological fantasy with unusually thoughtful characters.

  7. Spinning Silver — Naomi Novik

    Rumpelstiltskin retelling set in a thinly fictionalised Lithuania. Three women's voices, each clearly distinct. The romance is a quieter beat than the worldbuilding.

  8. Gideon the Ninth — Tamsyn Muir

    First book of the Locked Tomb. A bone-magic swordswoman gets dragged to a haunted palace. Funnier than it sounds.

  9. Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell — Susanna Clarke

    English magicians during the Napoleonic Wars. 800 pages of slow, footnoted, very dry magic. Polarising and rewarding.

  10. The Priory of the Orange Tree — Samantha Shannon

    Standalone epic fantasy with dragons, queens, and a refreshing absence of trilogy padding. Worldbuilding leans on East and South Asian sources rather than the usual European default.

I'll keep updating this as I actually read them. Right now I'm in the middle of The Goblin Emperor and it's living up to the recommendation.
